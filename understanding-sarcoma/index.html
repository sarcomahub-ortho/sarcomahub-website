Understanding sarcoma

A plain-language guide to bone and soft tissue tumours, written for patients and families.

What is sarcoma?

Sarcoma is a cancer that starts in the body’s connective tissues: bone, cartilage, muscle, fat, nerves, blood vessels and the deep tissues that hold everything together. It can appear almost anywhere, but it is most common in the arms and legs.

There are two main groups. Bone sarcomas start in the bone itself. Soft tissue sarcomas start in the soft tissues around it. Not every bone or soft tissue tumour is cancer; many are benign, but some benign tumours still need specialist care.

Because sarcoma is rare and has many subtypes, it is best looked after by a specialist team that sees many patients with it.

Common types

Osteosarcoma

The most common bone cancer. Usually affects teenagers and young adults, often around the knee or shoulder.

Ewing sarcoma

A bone or soft tissue cancer seen mostly in children and young people, often in the pelvis, legs or chest wall.

Chondrosarcoma

Starts in cartilage. More common in adults, often in the pelvis, thigh bone or shoulder.

Soft tissue sarcomas

A large family that includes liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma.

Rhabdomyosarcoma

The most common soft tissue sarcoma in children. It starts in cells that would normally become muscle.

Giant cell tumour of bone

Usually benign, but it can damage the bone near a joint and come back, so it is treated by a bone tumour team.

Warning signs

Sarcoma often causes few symptoms at first. A painless lump is easy to ignore, and bone pain can be mistaken for a sports injury or growing pains. See a doctor if you notice any of these:

  • A lump that is growing, is larger than 5 cm, or lies deep in the muscle
  • A lump that comes back after it was removed
  • Bone pain that does not settle, or is worse at night
  • Swelling or stiffness near a joint, or a limp without a clear cause
  • A bone that breaks after a minor fall

Please do not have a lump cut out before it is properly investigated. A sarcoma removed without planning can make later surgery much harder. An MRI and a planned biopsy come first.

How sarcoma is diagnosed

  1. Consultation and examinationA specialist asks about your symptoms and examines the area.
  2. X-ray and MRIAn X-ray shows the bone. An MRI shows the tumour’s size and how close it is to nerves, vessels and joints.
  3. BiopsyA small sample is taken with a needle, ideally by the team who will do the surgery, so the path can be removed later.
  4. Staging scansA CT scan of the chest, and sometimes a PET-CT or bone scan, checks whether the disease has spread.
  5. Tumour boardSurgeons, oncologists, radiologists and pathologists agree on a treatment plan together.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the type, size, grade and location of the tumour. Most people have a combination of treatments.

TreatmentWhat it does
SurgeryRemoves the tumour with a margin of healthy tissue. Most patients today keep their limb (limb salvage), with the bone rebuilt using a metal implant or bone graft.
ChemotherapyMedicines that kill cancer cells. Central to treating osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma, often given before and after surgery.
RadiotherapyHigh-energy beams aimed at the tumour. Often used for soft tissue sarcomas and Ewing sarcoma, before or after surgery.
Targeted therapy and trialsNewer medicines for some subtypes. Your team can tell you if a clinical trial may suit you.

After treatment

Regular check-ups, usually every few months at first, look for any return of the disease. Physiotherapy helps you regain strength and movement, and many people return to school, work and sport. Emotional support matters just as much, for patients and for the people who care for them.

Common questions

Is every lump a sarcoma?

No. Most lumps are benign, such as lipomas or cysts. But a lump that is large, deep or growing should be checked with an MRI.

Will I lose my arm or leg?

For most patients, no. With modern surgery the large majority keep their limb. Your surgeon will explain the options for your situation.

Why should I see a sarcoma specialist?

Sarcoma is rare, and outcomes are better when a team that treats it regularly plans the biopsy, surgery and other treatment from the start.

Can sarcoma be cured?

Many sarcomas can be cured, especially when found early and treated by a specialist team. Your doctors can explain what to expect for your type and stage.
