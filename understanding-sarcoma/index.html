Understanding sarcoma

A plain-language guide to bone and soft tissue tumours, written for patients and families.

What is sarcoma?

Sarcoma is a cancer that starts in the body’s connective tissues: bone, cartilage, muscle, fat, nerves, blood vessels and the deep tissues that hold everything together. It can appear almost anywhere, but it is most common in the arms and legs.

There are two main groups. Bone sarcomas start in the bone itself. Soft tissue sarcomas start in the soft tissues around it, and each one is named after the tissue it arises from: liposarcoma from fat, leiomyosarcoma from smooth muscle, chondrosarcoma from cartilage, and so on. Not every bone or soft tissue tumour is cancer; many are benign, but some benign tumours still need specialist care.

Because sarcoma is rare and has many subtypes, it is best looked after by a specialist team that sees many patients with it.

Common types

Some of these are cancers (malignant) and some are not (benign). All of them are best assessed by a bone and soft tissue tumour team.

Osteosarcoma

The most common bone cancer. Usually affects teenagers and young adults, often around the knee or shoulder.

Ewing sarcoma

A bone or soft tissue cancer seen mostly in children and young people, often in the pelvis, legs or chest wall.

Chondrosarcoma

Starts in cartilage. More common in adults, often in the pelvis, thigh bone or shoulder.

Soft tissue sarcomas

A large family named after the tissue they start in, including liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma.

Giant cell tumour of bone

Usually benign, but it can damage the bone near a joint and come back, so it is treated by a bone tumour team.

Other benign bone tumours

Common in everyday practice: aneurysmal bone cyst, simple (unicameral) bone cyst and osteoid osteoma. They are not cancer, but some weaken the bone or cause pain and need treatment.

Metastasis to bone

When a cancer from another organ, such as the breast, prostate, lung, kidney or thyroid, spreads and settles in the bone. This is very common, far more common than cancers that start in bone.

Myeloma

A cancer of the plasma cells in the bone marrow. It often affects the spine, pelvis and ribs, and can weaken bones so they break more easily.

Warning signs

Sarcoma often causes few symptoms at first. A painless lump is easy to ignore, and bone pain can be mistaken for a sports injury or growing pains. See a doctor if you notice any of these red flags:

  • A lump that is growing, is larger than 5 cm, or lies deep in the muscle
  • A lump that comes back after it was removed
  • Bone pain that does not settle, or is worse at night
  • Swelling or stiffness near a joint, or a limp without a clear cause
  • A bone that breaks after a minor fall

Please do not have a lump cut out before it is properly investigated. A sarcoma removed without planning can make later surgery much harder. An MRI and a planned biopsy come first.

How sarcoma is diagnosed

  1. Consultation and examinationA specialist asks about your symptoms and examines the area.
  2. X-ray and MRIAn X-ray shows the bone. An MRI shows the tumour’s size and how close it is to nerves, vessels and joints.
  3. BiopsyA small sample is taken with a needle, ideally by the team who will do the surgery. More about biopsy
  4. Staging scansA CT scan of the chest, and sometimes a PET-CT or bone scan, checks whether the disease has spread.
  5. Tumour boardSurgeons, oncologists, radiologists and pathologists agree on a treatment plan together.
A closer look

Biopsy

What a biopsy is

A biopsy takes a small piece of the tumour so it can be studied under a microscope. That piece is called tissue: a group of cells that make up a part of the body, such as bone, muscle or fat. Scans show where a tumour is and how big it is, but only the tissue can tell us exactly what kind of tumour it is. That answer decides the whole treatment plan.

Types of biopsy

  • Core needle biopsy: the most common. A hollow needle takes a few thin cores of tissue, usually guided by ultrasound or CT, through a tiny cut.
  • Open biopsy: a small, planned operation to take a larger piece, used when a needle cannot get enough tissue.

What happens to the tissue

The sample goes to the laboratory, where it is processed and examined under a microscope. Special stains (immunohistochemistry) and sometimes genetic or molecular tests help identify the exact type and grade of the tumour.

Why an oncopathologist

Sarcomas are rare and come in many subtypes that can look alike. An oncopathologist, a pathologist who specialises in tumours, sees them often and is more likely to give an accurate diagnosis.

Why the treating surgeon should plan it

The track of the biopsy needle can carry tumour cells, so it is removed later along with the tumour. The surgeon who will do the operation knows where the final cut will be and places the biopsy along that line. A badly placed biopsy can make limb-saving surgery harder.

How long results take

Usually about 5 to 10 working days. Special stains or molecular tests can take longer. Your team will tell you when to expect the report.

How it is done

A needle biopsy is usually a day procedure under local anaesthesia, sometimes with mild sedation. Children, and biopsies in deep areas such as the pelvis or spine, may need a short general anaesthetic. An open biopsy is done in the operating theatre under anaesthesia.

Do’s and don’ts after a biopsy

  • Keep the dressing clean and dry for 2 to 3 days
  • Take the pain relief your team recommends
  • Avoid heavy lifting or strenuous activity with that limb until you are told it is safe; if the tumour is in a bone of the leg, use crutches if advised
  • Contact your team if you notice fever, spreading redness, bleeding or a sudden increase in pain
  • Do not massage the area or apply heat or local remedies

Treatment

Treatment depends on the type, size, grade and location of the tumour. Most people have a combination of treatments.

Surgery

Removes the tumour with a margin of healthy tissue. Most patients today keep their limb (limb salvage), with the bone rebuilt using a metal implant or bone graft. More about surgery

Chemotherapy

Medicines that kill cancer cells throughout the body. For tumours such as osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma, chemotherapy is often given before surgery: it kills cancer cells, shrinks the tumour and makes its edges firmer, so it is easier to remove safely with the limb saved. It also treats tiny deposits elsewhere that scans cannot see. More chemotherapy usually follows after surgery.

Radiotherapy

High-energy beams aimed at the tumour. Often used for soft tissue sarcomas and Ewing sarcoma, before or after surgery.

Targeted therapy and trials

Newer medicines for some subtypes. Your team can tell you if a clinical trial may suit you.

The heart of treatment

Surgery for bone and soft tissue tumours

Surgery is the main treatment for most bone and soft tissue tumours. How the tumour is removed, and how the limb is rebuilt afterwards, makes a lasting difference to how well the person can walk, work and play for years to come.

Two main approaches

Intralesional surgery

Curettage and extended curettage, for benign and locally aggressive tumours such as bone cysts and giant cell tumour.

The surgeon makes a small window in the bone, goes inside the tumour and scrapes it out. In extended curettage, the walls of the cavity are then treated with a high-speed burr and sometimes heat, cold or chemicals to kill any cells left behind. The space is filled with bone graft or bone cement.

It is safe to enter these tumours because they do not spread through the body, so opening them does not seed cancer elsewhere. This lets the surgeon keep the patient’s own bone and joint.

En bloc or wide excision

For more aggressive and malignant tumours, such as osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, chondrosarcoma and soft tissue sarcomas.

En bloc means “in one piece”. In a wide excision, the tumour is removed in one piece together with a cuff of healthy tissue all around it, called the margin. The tumour itself is never opened or seen during the operation.

A malignant tumour must not be cut into, because its cells can spill into the wound and grow again. The choice between approaches depends on the tumour type, how it looks under the microscope (its grade) and where it is in the body.

What should not happen

Two avoidable problems can change the outcome of a sarcoma:

  • An unplanned intralesional procedure: a malignant tumour is opened or “shelled out” because it was thought to be benign, often without an MRI or biopsy first.
  • A positive margin: tumour cells are left at the edge of what was removed.

Either can lead to the tumour coming back and spreading locally into the surrounding tissue. A second, much bigger operation is then needed, often with radiotherapy, and the chance of saving the limb falls. This is why every suspicious lump should be investigated and operated on by a specialist team.

Reconstruction after surgery

When a section of bone or a joint is removed, it has to be rebuilt. There are several ways to do this, and each has strengths and limits:

Megaprosthesis (endoprosthesis)

A modular metal implant that replaces the removed bone and the joint next to it.

Advantages: early weight-bearing and walking, a predictable result, and growing (expandable) versions for children.

Disadvantages: it can loosen, wear or become infected over the years, and may need revision surgery.

Biological reconstruction

Uses bone: the patient’s own tumour bone after it is treated to kill the cancer cells and put back (recycled autograft), donor bone (allograft), or a living bone graft such as the fibula.

Advantages: once healed it becomes part of the body and can last a lifetime, and the patient’s own joint can often be kept.

Disadvantages: healing takes months, with protected walking, and there is a risk of fracture or the graft not joining.

Combinations and custom implants

A graft and an implant used together, or an implant made for one patient, combine the strengths of each method.

Advantages: fits difficult areas such as the pelvis, and can save the patient’s own joint.

Disadvantages: needs detailed planning, specialist expertise and time to make.

Every case is different. The right reconstruction depends on the person’s age and activity, the bone involved, how much is removed, whether the joint can be kept, and the other treatments planned. What suits one patient may not suit another, so the plan is made for each person.

Recent advances in reconstruction

3D planning, 3D printing and custom implants

Using the patient’s CT and MRI scans, the surgery can be planned on a 3D model of their own bone. Patient-specific cutting guides help the surgeon remove the tumour with the planned margin while keeping as much healthy bone as possible. Custom 3D-printed implants are then made to fit the exact gap, with a porous lattice surface that the patient’s bone can grow into.

This matters most in difficult places such as the pelvis, the shoulder and around the joints, and when a child’s growth plate or joint can be saved. A precise fit gives a more stable reconstruction and better movement.

Our team trained at leading centres around the world, and can offer any form of reconstruction done anywhere in the world, using 3D printing, 3D planning and custom implants.

Why advanced surgery matters today

Surgeons have performed limb-saving surgery for many years without these technologies, and it has helped many people. Today the goal is higher: not only to save the limb, but to give it the best possible function for the longest possible time.

A reconstruction that only works well for one or two years is not enough for a young person with decades ahead of them. Better planning, better-fitting implants and biological methods that become part of the body all aim for a reconstruction that lasts, so the person can walk, work, play and live independently for years to come.

After treatment

Regular check-ups, usually every few months at first, look for any return of the disease. Physiotherapy helps you regain strength and movement, and many people return to school, work and sport. Emotional support matters just as much, for patients and for the people who care for them.

Common questions

Is every lump a sarcoma?

No. Most lumps are benign, such as lipomas or cysts. But a lump that is large, deep or growing should be checked with an MRI.

Will I lose my arm or leg?

For most patients, no. With modern surgery the large majority keep their limb. Your surgeon will explain the options for your situation.

Why should I see a sarcoma specialist?

Sarcoma is rare, and outcomes are better when a team that treats it regularly plans the biopsy, surgery and other treatment from the start.

Can sarcoma be cured?

Many sarcomas can be cured, especially when found early and treated by a specialist team. Your doctors can explain what to expect for your type and stage.

Does a biopsy make the cancer spread?

No, not when it is planned properly. The biopsy track is placed where the surgeon will remove it later with the tumour.

Is the biopsy painful?

It is done under local or general anaesthesia, so you should feel little during it. Mild soreness for a few days afterwards is common.

Why do I need an MRI before surgery?

The MRI shows the tumour’s exact size and its relation to nerves, vessels and joints, which the surgeon needs to plan a safe operation.

How long does treatment take?

For many bone sarcomas, chemotherapy and surgery together take about 6 to 9 months. Benign tumours often need only one operation.

Can my child still grow normally after surgery?

Often yes. Growing implants and joint-saving techniques let the limb keep pace as the child grows, sometimes with small follow-up procedures.

Will I be able to walk after surgery?

Most people walk again, often within days to weeks after a metal implant, and over a few months after a bone graft heals.

Can a benign tumour turn into cancer?

Very rarely. Most benign tumours stay benign, but some need treatment because they weaken the bone or come back.
