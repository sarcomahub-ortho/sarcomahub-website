For families and carers

When your child or loved one has sarcoma

Many families face this, and many come through it. Here is help with the questions families ask us most, from the first scan to life after treatment.

The first few weeks

Hearing the word cancer about someone you love is overwhelming. It is normal to feel shock, fear and guilt. Many parents tell us they wish they had known it was alright to ask the same question many times.

  • Keep a notebook or phone note for test dates, results and questions
  • Ask who to call, day or night, if your child has a fever during chemotherapy
  • Bring someone with you to important appointments
  • Ask about fertility preservation for teenagers before treatment starts

Talking with your child

Children cope better with simple, honest answers suited to their age. Let them ask questions, and tell them it is not their fault. Brothers and sisters need attention and honest answers too.

Is it inherited?

Almost never. It is extremely rare for a child’s bone or soft tissue cancer to be passed down from a parent. Nothing you did, ate or missed caused it, and it is very unlikely that brothers and sisters are at risk.

In a very small number of families, a known genetic condition raises the risk. If your team thinks this is possible, they will talk to you about it and arrange the right tests. Otherwise, there is no need for the rest of the family to be screened.

A long journey, one step at a time

Treatment for a malignant bone tumour such as osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma is not like routine bone surgery. It usually takes about 6 to 9 months. Parents, the child and the team get ready together, and then take it one day at a time.

You will have a clear roadmap from the start: how many cycles of chemotherapy, when the surgery will be, and what kind of surgery is planned. There is plenty of discussion between the treating team and the family along the way, so staying in close touch with your doctors really matters.

After treatment ends, regular follow-up continues for about 5 years, with check-ups more often at first and less often as time goes on.

Life after surgery

The first thing parents want to know is whether the cancer can be cured. When it is found early and has not spread, the outlook is good for many children, and today the focus is also on how well they will live afterwards.

As a general guide, most children are up and walking with support within days to a few weeks of surgery. Over the next few months they become independent with daily activities and return to school. Routine activities like cycling usually follow within several months, and more demanding activities like sports can often be returned to later, once the bone and muscles are strong.

The exact timeline depends on the tumour, the bone involved and the type of surgery, and your team will discuss it with you for your child.

Counselling support

Cancer affects the whole family. Counselling with a psychologist can be arranged for the patient and for family members, at any stage. Asking for it is a sign of strength, not weakness, and many families find it helps them stay steady through treatment.

Keep the decision circle small

There is no need to discuss the diagnosis with everyone in the family. Well-meant advice from many people can be confusing. It works best when a few main decision-makers stay close to the team and share news with others as they choose.

Families need to prepare in three ways: emotionally, for the months ahead; financially, for treatment and travel; and practically, for who will stay with the patient, look after other children and keep home and work going.

For adults with another cancer

Metastasis: cancer spread to bone

What it is. Metastasis means a cancer that started somewhere else in the body, such as the breast, prostate, lung, kidney or thyroid, has spread and settled in the bone.

How common it is. Very common, and far more common than cancers that start in the bone. Many people living with cancer will have it at some point, and with today’s treatments many of them live well for years.

Why to watch for it. If you or someone you love has had cancer, look out for new bone pain that does not go away, pain at night, or a limp. If it is missed, the bone can weaken and break, causing severe pain and making it hard to walk or care for oneself. Found early, it is much easier to treat.

The surgeries we perform

  • Strengthening a weak bone with a rod or plate before it breaks
  • Fixing a bone that has already broken
  • Removing the affected bone and replacing it with a joint or bone implant
  • Stabilising and relieving pressure in the spine
  • Cement and other minimally invasive procedures for pain

What surgery aims for

The aim is function and comfort. Whatever the original cancer, if the person is living well and responding to treatment, surgery aims to relieve pain and keep them on their feet: walking, doing their day-to-day work and living independently.

Surgery works alongside the person’s cancer treatment, such as radiotherapy and medicines, and the teams plan it together.
